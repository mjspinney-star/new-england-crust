We have cooked on a lot of surfaces. Cast iron pans, perforated pans, dark nonstick pans with ridges, sheet trays, and stones that cost less than a large pepperoni from a decent pizzeria. The stone wins. Every time.

That is not a hot take — it is just physics.

## What a Pan Actually Does

Most pizza pans are designed for convenience, not performance. They are easy to clean, easy to store, and easy to sell. What they are not good at is holding heat long enough to do anything useful to the bottom of your pizza.

You slide a dressed pie onto a cold or lukewarm pan and the bottom steams more than it bakes. You get a cooked top and a pale, soft undercarriage. It is edible. It is not pizza.

**Thermal mass** is the thing that matters here. A stone — even a thin, budget cordierite stone — absorbs heat during a long preheat and then dumps that heat directly into the bottom of your dough the moment it makes contact. That is how you get a proper crust.

## The Case for Cheap

Here is the part people find surprising: you do not need to spend much. A basic cordierite stone in the $20 to $35 range does the job. Cordierite handles thermal shock well, which matters if you are cooking outdoors in October and your stone is sitting in a cold oven while you fire it up.

We have cracked stones before. Usually because we got impatient, skipped the slow preheat, and put a cold stone directly over a high flame. That is user error, not a material failure — and it happens with cheap stones and expensive ones equally.

The pricier stones — thicker slabs, baking steels — do hold heat longer and recover faster between cooks. If you are running back-to-back pies for six people, that matters. For a weeknight cook with one or two pizzas, a cheap stone does the same job.

## What a Pan Is Actually Good For

We are not throwing pans out entirely. A well-oiled pan is the right call for a Detroit-style or a deep-dish. The walls matter there. But for any pizza where you want a thin, crisp bottom and a real crust — the kind you get in the first 90 seconds inside an outdoor oven — a pan gets in the way.

The stone is the right tool for the right job. The pan is a different job entirely.

## One Thing to Know Before You Buy

Preheat time is non-negotiable. A stone that has only been in the oven for 15 minutes is not ready. We aim for 45 minutes minimum in a home oven at 500°F. Use an **infrared thermometer** to read the surface before you launch — you want to see 500°F or hotter on the stone itself, not just the oven dial.

A cold stone and a hot oven are not the same thing.

## Try This Next

If you already have a stone but have been skipping the long preheat, run one cook with a full 45-minute warmup and compare it to what you have been getting. The difference in the bottom crust will tell you everything you need to know.
