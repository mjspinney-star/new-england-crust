We have gone through some genuinely expensive olive oil on pizzas that did not need it at all. That took a few cooks to figure out.

Finishing olive oil — the good stuff, cold-pressed, grassy or peppery, the kind that costs real money per bottle — is not a universal upgrade. It depends entirely on what you are putting it on and when you are applying it.

## What "Finishing Oil" Actually Means Here

We are not talking about the olive oil you mix into dough or brush on a par-baked crust. That is cooking oil. **Finishing oil** is what you drizzle on after the pizza comes out of the oven — or right before the last fifteen seconds of the cook. It never sees serious heat. The point is that you actually taste it.

If it burns off, you wasted it. High heat — the kind your outdoor oven runs at — destroys delicate flavor compounds fast.

## When It Genuinely Matters

A Margherita is the clearest example. Simple ingredients, not much going on flavor-wise, and a good drizzle after the cut adds something you can actually taste. Same goes for a white pie with ricotta and not much else on it.

Anything where bread and cheese are doing most of the work benefits from finishing oil. It adds a little bitterness, a little pepper, rounds out the richness. You notice it.

A plain focaccia-style dough baked on the stone — no sauce, maybe some flaky salt — is another spot where finishing oil earns its place.

## When It Does Not Matter

Load a pizza up with fennel sausage, roasted peppers, and two cheeses, and that finishing oil is going to disappear. You will not taste it. Save it.

Same goes for anything heavily sauced. The acidity and sugar in tomato sauce will bury whatever nuance you are paying for in that bottle.

We have also found that on a busy pizza night — launching four or five pies back to back, everyone hungry, kids running around the backyard — nobody is noticing the finishing oil anyway. That is a weeknight reality. Use the regular stuff.

## A Note on Heat and Timing

If you want to use finishing oil but still want a little warmth in it, drizzle it on right as the pizza comes off the stone. The residual heat from the crust warms the oil without cooking it. That is the sweet spot — you get fragrance without losing the flavor.

Do not drizzle it on the peel before the launch. It will smoke, it will burn, and you will lose whatever you paid for.

## What to Actually Buy

You do not need to spend twenty dollars on a small bottle for this. A mid-range cold-pressed extra virgin — somewhere in the eight to twelve dollar range for a 500ml bottle — is enough to make a difference. Look for a harvest date on the label. If there is no harvest date, skip it.

We keep two bottles on hand: one decent everyday oil for dough and sauce work, one smaller bottle of something better for finishing. That separation alone changed how we use both.

**If the pizza has room for the oil to speak, use the good stuff. If it does not, do not bother.**

## Try This Next

Pick one straightforward pizza this week — a Margherita or a white pie — and finish it with a real drizzle right off the stone. Then make the same pizza without it. You will know within one bite whether it is worth keeping in the rotation.
