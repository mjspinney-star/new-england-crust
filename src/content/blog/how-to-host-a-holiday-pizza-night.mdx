Holiday pizza night sounds fun until you realize you're the one standing outside in 40°F weather while everyone else is warm and talking. A little planning up front makes the difference between a smooth evening and a chaotic mess of cold dough and impatient guests.

## Start the Dough Two Days Out

This is the most important deadline. If you're cold-fermenting — and you should be — the dough needs at least 24 hours in the fridge, and 48 hours gives you noticeably better flavor and a more relaxed, open dough. Our [72-hour cold-ferment dough guide](/blog/72-hour-cold-ferment-dough/) walks through the full process if you want to push it even further.

For a group of six to eight people, plan on six to eight dough balls at 250–300 grams each. That's two batches in the bread maker — mix the first on Thursday morning for a Saturday night, the second Thursday evening. Pull everything from the fridge about an hour before you start stretching so the dough relaxes and comes up closer to room temperature.

**Takeaway:** Dough timing is the one thing you can't improvise around. Set a calendar reminder.

## Build a Toppings Bar and Let People Load Their Own

Prepping toppings ahead cuts down on chaos during the cook. Small bowls work well — shredded cheese, sliced fresh mozzarella, torn basil, caramelized onions, whatever you're offering. If you need recipe inspiration beyond the classics, [our pizza night recipe roundup](/blog/pizza-night-recipes-beyond-margherita/) covers a range of combinations that hold up well for a group.

Keep wet ingredients — fresh tomatoes, anything high-moisture — in their own bowls so guests don't accidentally flood the dough before it launches. Let people top their own pizzas if you're up for a little chaos, or call out two or three set combinations and take orders.

**Takeaway:** Prepped toppings make the cook go faster. Keeping wet and dry ingredients separated stops the dough from going soggy before it hits the oven.

## Cold Weather Changes the Preheat — Plan for It

In New England, October nights get cold fast, and outdoor preheating takes longer than it does in July. The broader logistics of setting up for a group are covered in our [backyard pizza night setup post](/blog/backyard-pizza-night-setup/). The cold-weather short version: give the oven more time than you think it needs, and always confirm the stone temperature with an infrared thermometer before you launch the first pizza. Guessing gets you pale bottoms — we know this from the period before we owned one. Our post on [reading your stone with an infrared thermometer](/blog/2026-06-16-reading-your-stone-with-an-infrared-thermometer/) explains what to look for.

Our routine on the Ninja Woodfire is to preheat to 550°F — about 20 minutes under normal conditions, potentially longer when it's raw out — and confirm the stone reads close to 540°F. We brush the stretched dough with oil, par-bake for about a minute and a half, then add toppings and bake for roughly five more minutes. The result is an evenly golden crust with some blistering on the edge. Not leopard-spotted, but consistent and crowd-friendly.

If you want a faster cook, the 700°F setting brings a pizza in around three minutes — useful when you've got a line of people waiting.

**Takeaway:** Cold air slows your preheat. Check the stone temperature before every pie, not just the first one.

## Keep Pizzas Coming Without Bottlenecks

The bottleneck is always the oven, not the prep. A few things help:

- Have the next dough ball stretched and waiting on a floured surface before the current pizza is done.
- Keep the toppings bar within arm's reach of the peel so loading goes fast.
- Designate one person to manage launches and pulls so the oven isn't standing open longer than necessary.

If you're using semolina on the peel — and it's worth considering — [this post on why semolina works differently than flour](/blog/2026-07-09-the-case-for-semolina-on-the-peel-what-it-does-tha/) explains the logic. A stuck pizza during a group cook is the most stressful thing to troubleshoot mid-evening.

**Takeaway:** Stretch ahead, load fast, and have one person running the oven. Three people hovering around the peel at once rarely ends well.
