A soggy bottom is probably the most common complaint in backyard pizza. You pull the pie, the top looks great, and then you lift a slice and the whole thing droops. Before you blame your dough or your oven, it helps to narrow down which part of the process is actually causing the problem — because there are a few, and they stack.

## Start with the stone temperature

This is the most likely culprit. If your stone isn't hot enough when the pizza lands, the bottom steams instead of crisps. The oven air temperature and the stone surface temperature are not the same thing. An oven that reads 550°F may have a stone that's still 50 or 60 degrees behind — especially if you haven't preheated long enough.

The only reliable way to know what your stone is actually doing is an infrared thermometer. We talk about exactly how to use one in [our guide to reading your pizza stone with an infrared thermometer](/blog/2026-06-16-reading-your-stone-with-an-infrared-thermometer/). The short version: don't guess, and don't rush the preheat. Most outdoor ovens need more time than you'd expect.

## Watch your sauce volume

A heavy sauce layer is one of the fastest ways to introduce moisture the bottom can't escape fast enough. The sauce doesn't need to be thick — it needs to be right-sized. If you're using a cooked sauce that has a lot of liquid, reduce it. If you're using canned tomatoes blended raw, strain off some of that liquid before you spread.

Our go-to is a [no-cook tomato sauce](/blog/a-no-cook-tomato-sauce-that-does-not-need/) — it's thick enough that you aren't fighting extra moisture from the start. Use a light hand when you spread it. The sauce should cover the dough, not pool on it.

## Wet toppings are a real problem

Fresh mozzarella, canned mushrooms, roasted vegetables with residual liquid — anything that holds water will release it during the bake. That moisture goes straight down into the dough. A few habits help:

Tear fresh mozzarella and let it drain on a paper towel for 20–30 minutes before it goes on the pie. If you're using mushrooms, cook them off first so they've already released their water. Roasted vegetables should be patted dry. None of this is complicated, but skipping it shows up clearly in the finished crust.

## Par-baking makes a real difference

On our Ninja Woodfire — preheated to 550°F with the stone reading around 540°F — we brush the stretched dough with oil and par-bake it for about 1 minute 30 seconds before any toppings go on. That short run gives the bottom a head start before the sauce and cheese add their moisture load. We cover our full routine in [how we cook pizza on the Ninja Woodfire](/blog/how-we-cook-pizza-on-the-ninja-woodfire/), if you want all the details in one place. After par-baking, toppings go on and the pie goes back in for about 5 minutes.

If your setup allows for a hot stone and a quick pre-bake of the crust, it's worth trying. It changes what the dough is doing when the toppings land on it.

## The dough itself matters too

A well-fermented dough handles oven heat differently than one that hasn't had enough time. A cold ferment develops structure and flavor, and that structure holds up better under toppings. We use a [72-hour cold-ferment dough](/blog/72-hour-cold-ferment-dough/) as our standard, and the difference between that and a rushed same-day dough is noticeable in how the bottom behaves.

If you're consistently getting soggy results even with a hot stone and controlled toppings, check your fermentation time. Underfermented dough can be gummy in the center no matter what else you do right.

## The fix is usually a combination

Soggy bottoms rarely come from one single mistake. More often it's a stone that wasn't quite hot enough, a little too much sauce, and fresh mozzarella that wasn't drained — all at once. Work through each variable one at a time and the problem usually clears up fast.
