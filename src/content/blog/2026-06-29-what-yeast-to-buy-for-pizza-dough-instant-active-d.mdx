Most people grab whatever yeast is on the shelf and hope for the best. That works — until it doesn't. A slow, sluggish dough in November or an overproofed mess in July usually traces back to the yeast, not the recipe.

Short answer first: we use **Caputo Lievito**, a dry yeast made by the same Italian company that mills our 00 flour. It goes straight into the bread maker with everything else — no proofing — and it holds up well through a 24–48 hour cold ferment. Here's how the three main types compare, and why we landed there.

## Instant Yeast: The Easiest Option on the Shelf

**Instant yeast** — sometimes labeled rapid-rise or fast-acting — goes straight into dry flour without proofing first. No waiting, no dissolving in water, no extra bowl to wash.

It ferments reliably and works for both same-day doughs and long cold ferments.

One note: the "rapid-rise" labeling on some brands is marketing for breads that need one rise. For pizza, you are controlling fermentation time yourself anyway. Just use it like standard instant yeast and ignore the packaging claims.

**Verdict: The simplest choice if you're buying yeast at a regular grocery store. SAF Red is a widely available brand.**

## Active Dry Yeast: The Traditional Choice

**Active dry yeast** has larger granules and was the standard for decades. The traditional instruction is to proof it in warm water, around 100 to 110°F, for about ten minutes before mixing. Too hot and you kill it; too cool and it's slow to wake up. In a cold New England kitchen in March, water cools faster than you expect.

Not every dry yeast needs that step. Caputo Lievito, the one we use, is labeled simply "dry yeast," and its directions say to mix it with the flour before adding the other ingredients — no dissolving in water first. In our bread maker it goes in last, on top of the flour.

**Verdict: Check the label. If it says to proof first, do it. If it says it can go straight into the flour, skip the extra bowl.**

## Fresh Yeast: Worth Knowing About

**Fresh yeast** — also called cake yeast or compressed yeast — is what a lot of Neapolitan pizzerias use. It is highly active, has a mild, slightly milky smell, and produces a dough with noticeably good flavor when used in long ferments.

The catch: it has a shelf life of about two weeks, it is hard to find outside of specialty grocery stores or restaurant suppliers, and it crumbles unevenly if you do not know what you are doing with it.

**Verdict: Interesting to try if you find it, but not a practical everyday ingredient for most backyard cooks.**

## What We Use: Caputo Lievito

Our everyday dough is the [Caputo 00 Breadmaker Dough](/recipes/caputo-breadmaker-dough/): 500 g Caputo 00 flour, 325 g cool water, 13 g fine sea salt, and 1.5 g of Caputo Lievito. That's not much yeast — the 24–48 hours in the fridge does most of the work.
