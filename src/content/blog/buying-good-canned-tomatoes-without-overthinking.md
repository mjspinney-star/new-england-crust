We have stood in the grocery aisle holding two cans of tomatoes for way too long. Both look fine. One costs $1.79, one costs $4.50. The label on the expensive one says something about a volcanic region in Italy. The cheap one just says "whole peeled tomatoes."

Here is what we have figured out after running both through a lot of pizzas.

## The Label Tells You Most of What You Need

Flip the can over and read the ingredients. Good canned tomatoes have two or three items on that list: tomatoes, tomato juice or puree, maybe salt. That is it. If you see citric acid, that is fine — it is just a preservative and it does not affect flavor much. If you see tomato paste or "tomato concentrate" listed before juice, the manufacturer is bulking up a lower-quality product. Put it back.

Sodium content matters too. Some brands salt heavily, which limits how much control you have over your sauce. Look for something under 200mg per half-cup serving if you want to season it yourself.

## Whole Peeled vs. Crushed vs. Diced

For pizza, **whole peeled tomatoes** are the move. They are the least processed version, which means the manufacturer had less opportunity to mask mediocre tomatoes with seasoning or extra liquid. You crush them yourself — by hand or with an immersion blender — and you control the texture.

Crushed tomatoes are a reasonable shortcut. Diced tomatoes are treated with calcium chloride to hold their shape, which means they do not break down properly. Skip diced entirely for sauce.

## San Marzano: Worth It or Marketing?

D.O.P. **San Marzano tomatoes** — certified ones, grown in the Agro Sarnese-Nocerino region near Naples — are genuinely different. Lower acidity, meatier flesh, less water. If the can has the D.O.P. seal and "Pomodoro S. Marzano dell'Agro Sarnese-Nocerino" printed on it, you are getting the real thing. That is not a label you can fake legally.

A lot of cans just say "San Marzano style" or "San Marzano type." Those are domestic tomatoes grown to approximate the variety. They are often decent — but do not pay a premium for them.

For a weeknight pizza, a good domestic whole peeled tomato works. For something you are putting real time into — a long ferment dough, the good mozzarella — the certified D.O.P. can is worth it.

## Honest Assessment

The quality gap between a bad canned tomato and a good one is real, but it is not enormous. Bad tomatoes taste flat and metallic. Good tomatoes taste bright and clean. For most outdoor pizza sessions, a mid-range domestic brand with a short ingredient list will do the job. Save the D.O.P. cans for when the rest of your cook is dialed in.

The label does not lie if you know what to look for — short ingredient list, whole peeled, and either a legitimate D.O.P. seal or honest domestic labeling.

## Try This Next

Grab two cans — one domestic whole peeled, one D.O.P. San Marzano — and make a small batch of each side by side. Crush them by hand, hit both with a pinch of salt, and taste them raw before they go anywhere near the oven. You will understand the difference in about thirty seconds.
