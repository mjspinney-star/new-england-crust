Too much sauce is one of the most common reasons a pizza comes out soggy — and it's also one of the easiest problems to fix. The fix isn't a trick. It's just using less than you think you need, and spreading it thinner than feels natural the first time you do it.

## The number most people get wrong

For a 10–12 inch pizza, most cooks use somewhere between 3 and 4 ounces of sauce — roughly a generous quarter cup. That probably sounds like less than you're imagining. It is. The goal is a thin, even layer that covers the dough without pooling anywhere. If the sauce is thick enough that you can see it sitting on top of the dough as a distinct layer, you're likely over it.

A good test: after you spread it, you should be able to see the dough's texture through the sauce in places. If the surface looks like a solid red plane, pull some back.

## Consistency matters as much as quantity

Watery sauce causes as much trouble as too much sauce. If your tomatoes are very loose, the extra liquid will soak into the dough during baking and you'll lose the bottom. The usual advice is to strain crushed or whole tomatoes before you use them, or simply choose a brand that's not swimming in liquid to start. We have a whole post on [how to pick good canned tomatoes without overthinking it](/blog/buying-good-canned-tomatoes-without-overthinking/) if you want to get into that decision.

For a no-cook sauce, the approach is straightforward: crush or blend your tomatoes, season them, and use them as-is — no simmering needed. Our [no-cook tomato sauce](/blog/a-no-cook-tomato-sauce-that-does-not-need/) stays thick enough to spread without weeping because we're not adding any extra liquid in the first place.

The takeaway: a thicker sauce in smaller quantity beats a loose sauce in any quantity.

## How to spread it

Use the back of a spoon. Start at the center and work outward in slow, overlapping circles, stopping about an inch from the edge. You're not painting — you're nudging the sauce across the surface with light pressure. If you press hard, you risk tearing the dough and creating thin spots where liquid will collect.

Don't go to the edge. That uncovered border is what becomes the cornicione. If sauce reaches it, it steams instead of browning and you lose the texture that makes the edge worth eating.

One thin pass is usually enough. Resist the urge to go back for a second lap.

## Different styles, different amounts

Not every pie gets the same treatment. A New Haven–style tomato pie — like our [New Haven plain tomato pie](/recipes/new-haven-plain-tomato-pie/) — is intentionally saucier and drier in texture, with no cheese to absorb liquid. A New York slice uses a little more sauce than a Neapolitan but still less than most people assume. Bar-style pies like our [South Shore bar pizza](/recipes/south-shore-bar-pizza/) go edge to edge with sauce because the pan style and the crispier dough can handle it.

The style you're making should guide your amount. When in doubt, start with less. You can always add a small spoonful after the first attempt. You can't take it back once it's baked.

## What this looks like on the Ninja

We preheat to 550°F, confirm the stone reads around 540°F with an infrared thermometer, then brush the stretched dough with oil and [par-bake it for about 90 seconds](/blog/how-we-cook-pizza-on-the-ninja-woodfire/) before adding toppings. At that point the crust has set slightly, which means sauce applied after the par-bake sits on a sturdier surface and has less time to soak in. It's one reason we like the par-bake step — the dough isn't raw and porous when the sauce goes on. The full bake after toppings runs about 5 minutes at 550°F.

If the stone temperature is off when you start, all the sauce discipline in the world won't save a wet bottom. That's worth checking — our post on [reading your stone with an infrared thermometer](/blog/2026-06-16-reading-your-stone-with-an-infrared-thermometer/) covers exactly what to look for.
