For my first summer of backyard pizza, I made dough the day I baked it. Mix at noon, stretch at six, wonder at seven why the crust tasted like warm flour and fought me the whole way onto the peel.

The fix wasn't a better technique or a fancier flour. It was time. Once I started mixing dough on Tuesday for a Friday bake, everything downstream got easier — and I haven't made same-day dough on purpose since.

If you just want the method, it lives here: **[the New England Crust master dough](/recipes/master-cold-ferment-dough/)** — that's the canonical recipe I keep updated, with exact weights and steps. This post is the why, and what I've learned running the long version of it for two summers.

## What actually changed

Cold fermentation did three things for my pizza that nothing else had:

1. **Flavor.** Tang, depth, a touch of sweetness from broken-down starches. Same five ingredients, completely different crust.
2. **Extensibility.** The dough opens easily without springing back — which matters most at the worst moment, when you're stretching on a floured deck while a friend asks about toppings and the Ninja's pellet hopper is calling for a top-up.
3. **Schedule sanity.** Mixing on Tuesday means I'm not chained to a timer when guests arrive on Friday. The dough waits for me, not the other way around.

## The version I run at 72 hours

The [master dough](/recipes/master-cold-ferment-dough/) uses instant yeast and peaks at 48 hours — that's what I'd hand to anyone starting out. My Friday-night version pushes to 72 hours with sourdough levain instead. Baker's percentages, for six 260g balls:

- **Flour:** 1000g (80% bread flour, 20% 00 — I use <AffiliateLink id="caputo-flour" label="Antimo Caputo 00 Flour" />)
- **Water:** 660g (66%)
- **Salt:** 28g (2.8%)
- **Levain:** 100g active starter (10%)

No oil, no sugar, no commercial yeast. Mix and autolyse on day one, stretch and fold through the evening, bulk until visibly airy, then ball into oiled containers and straight to the fridge until pizza night.

The levain buys a little more tang and a more open crumb than the instant-yeast version — and costs you a starter to keep alive. If you don't already have one, start with the master dough. The gap between same-day and 48-hour dough is enormous; the gap between 48 and 72 with levain is a nice-to-have.

## What I learned baking it in two different ovens

The same dough behaves a little differently in each of my backyard ovens, and it took me most of a summer to stop fighting that:

- **In the Ooni (gas, deck at ~900°F):** stretch tight, top light, launch fast. The pie wants to be in and out in 60–75 seconds, turned at 30. The cornicione leopards hard.
- **In the Ninja Woodfire (pellets + electric, stone at ~700°F):** stretch a hair thicker, top a little more generously. The bake runs 4–5 minutes and the crust browns evenly with less leoparding — that's not a flaw, it's just the oven.

## The humidity lesson

The one that cost me the most ruined launches: if your kitchen is humid — August in coastal New England, say — drop the water by 2–3% and the dough handles dramatically better on the peel. Pizza is a humidity instrument as much as a flour one. Nobody's recipe card tells you that; two stuck pies in one night will.
