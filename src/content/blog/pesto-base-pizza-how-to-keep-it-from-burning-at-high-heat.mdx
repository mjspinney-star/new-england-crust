Pesto is one of those toppings that looks like a great idea right up until you pull a blackened, bitter pizza out of a 900-degree oven. We have made that mistake. The olive oil in pesto hits direct heat and goes dark fast, and once it burns, it is not coming back.

The fix is not complicated. But it does require you to stop treating pesto like a sauce.

## The Problem with Pesto as a Base

Most sauces — crushed tomato, white garlic oil — can take full contact with a screaming hot stone. Pesto cannot. The basil scorches, the oil separates, and the nuts toast past the point of good. At 850 to 950 degrees Fahrenheit, you have sixty to ninety seconds before everything goes wrong.

Pesto belongs on this pizza. It just does not belong in the oven.

## How We Build It

Start with a thin layer of olive oil directly on the stretched dough — just enough to coat the surface. Add your cheese. We use a mix of low-moisture mozzarella and a little **Parmigiano-Reggiano** grated over the top.

Launch the pizza without any pesto on it. Let it cook fully. Pull it when the crust is done and the cheese is bubbling and spotted.

Then — off the oven, off the stone, on a board — spoon or brush the pesto on while the pizza is still hot.

The residual heat does exactly enough. It loosens the pesto, lets it settle into the cheese, and warms the basil without burning a single leaf.

## What to Put Under the Pesto

A bare cheese base works, but we prefer adding something between the cheese and the finished pesto layer to give it more texture. A few options that hold up well:

- Thin-sliced fresh zucchini, laid flat before the cook — it softens without disappearing
- Cherry tomatoes, halved and placed cut-side up — they blister and concentrate
- A handful of **arugula** added at the same time as the pesto — it wilts slightly and balances the richness

None of these need to be pre-cooked. The oven handles them in the same window as the cheese.

## A Note on the Pesto Itself

Store-bought pesto works. We are not going to pretend otherwise. But if you have a basil plant that ran wild over August — and by mid-September in New England most of them have — this is the pizza that uses it up before the first frost kills everything.

If you are making it fresh, keep it thick. Thin pesto slides off hot cheese and puddles on the board. You want something that holds its shape when you spoon it.

## Real-World Quirk

If you are cooking on the Ooni Koda 12 or the Solo Stove Pi, the post-cook pesto method works without any modification. The Ninja Woodfire at 700 degrees Fahrenheit is a little more forgiving — pesto added at the last thirty seconds of the cook usually comes out fine rather than scorched — but we still prefer finishing off the heat.

Pesto does not need more time in the oven. It needs to be kept out of it.

## Try This Next

Make the pizza with just olive oil, mozzarella, and cherry tomatoes first. Pull it, taste a corner, then add the pesto to the rest. You will see — and taste — exactly what the heat does and does not need to do.
