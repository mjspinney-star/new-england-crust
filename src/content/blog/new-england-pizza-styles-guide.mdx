If you grew up in New England, you probably have a strong opinion about pizza — and a specific shop you'd defend in an argument. The region doesn't have one style. It has at least six worth knowing, and they don't taste anything like each other. Here's what makes each one distinct and where to start if you want to make them at home.

## New Haven Apizza

New Haven apizza starts with a coal-fired oven tradition that produces a thin, charred, almost crackerlike crust with serious chew at the edge. The plain tomato pie — mozzarella-free, just tomato and grated Romano — is the standard by which everything else gets measured. The crust has a tight crumb, dark spots, and a flavor that comes from long fermentation and very high heat.

Home ovens can't fully replicate a coal-fired result, but you can get a lot of the character right. Our [New Haven plain tomato pie](/recipes/new-haven-plain-tomato-pie/) focuses on the things you can control: flour, fermentation, and a hot stone. The takeaway: this style rewards patience more than equipment.

## South Shore Bar Pizza

The South Shore style — centered south of Boston — is the opposite of New Haven in almost every way. It's a small, round pan pizza, usually around ten inches, with a thin crust that crisps hard in the pan and a laced or "lacey" edge where the cheese runs over and browns against the sides. Sauce goes heavy, cheese goes to the very rim, and you eat the whole thing yourself.

It's a diner-counter pizza as much as it's a recipe. Our [South Shore bar pizza](/recipes/south-shore-bar-pizza/) leans into the cast-iron pan approach that gets that edge right. If you've never had one, the crust-to-cheese ratio is the first thing that surprises you.

## Greek Pizza

Greek pizza in New England — the kind you find at every "House of Pizza" from the South Shore to the North Shore — is thick, oily, and baked in a well-oiled pan. The bottom fries more than it bakes. The crust is chewy and soft, not crisp, with a dense crumb. Sauce tends to be sweeter and more heavily seasoned than a New York–style pie.

It's arguably the most underrated regional style in the country. Our [Greek pizza recipe](/recipes/greek-pizza/) uses a pan with a generous pour of oil in the bottom — that's not optional, it's the whole point. Takeaway: don't skip the oil, and don't rush the proof.

## Rhode Island Bakery Pizza

Rhode Island bakery pizza is a sheet-pan style sold by the strip at bakeries across the state — a thick, focaccia-adjacent dough topped simply with crushed tomatoes, olive oil, and sometimes Romano. It's sold at room temperature, cut into rectangles, and eaten as a snack as often as a meal.

It's one of the most unassuming styles on this list and one of the easiest to make at home. Our [Rhode Island bakery pizza](/recipes/rhode-island-bakery-pizza/) keeps the topping minimal on purpose. If you load it up, you've made something else. The restraint is the style.

## Beach Pizza

Beach pizza is a North Shore Massachusetts thing — specifically the kind served at clam shacks and beachside counters around Salisbury. It's thin, nearly cracker-crisp, cut into small squares, and served from a steam table or held in a warmer. It's not hot off the stone pizza. That's part of the character, not a flaw.

The sauce is tangy, the crust is thin and sturdy, and the whole thing is built to be eaten standing up. Our [Salisbury beach pizza](/recipes/beach-pizza/) gets into the details of why the cut and the brief rest matter. Takeaway: don't serve it immediately — let it sit for a minute the way it would at the counter.

## The Clam Pie

The white clam pie is the one New England style that has gone national, and it still gets misread. The real version is spare: olive oil, garlic, Pecorino, clams, sometimes fresh oregano. No mozzarella, no red sauce. The clam flavor has to stand on its own, which means it can't be buried.

Getting the clam-to-cheese ratio right is the whole challenge. Our [New England clam pie](/recipes/clam-pie-new-england-way/) goes into canned versus fresh and why each has a case — and if you want to dig deeper on that question, we wrote a full post on [canned vs. fresh clams for white clam pizza](/blog/2026-07-20-canned-vs-fresh-clams-for-new-england-white-clam-p/). Takeaway: less is more, and the garlic cannot be raw when it hits the oven.
