Mozzarella is probably the most common pizza cheese, and it's also the one most likely to leave you with a soggy puddle in the center of your pie. The fix isn't complicated, but it does require knowing which type you're working with and what it's going to do in a hot oven.

## The Short Version

Low-moisture mozzarella — the kind sold in blocks or pre-shredded bags — melts smoothly, browns lightly, and stays put. Fresh mozzarella — the soft white balls packed in water or whey — has a milder flavor and a creamier texture, but it releases a lot of moisture as it cooks. Use the wrong one for the wrong pie and you'll know immediately.

## Low-Moisture Mozzarella

This is the workhorse. It's been drained and dried during production, so there's not much water left to escape. It melts into an even layer, pulls into satisfying strings, and can handle a few extra minutes in the oven without turning into a soupy mess.

For most pizzas — anything with a loaded topping situation, any NY-style slice, bar pizza, grandma pies — low-moisture is the right call. Buy it in a block and shred it yourself if you can. Pre-shredded bags contain anti-caking agents that interfere with melting. It's a small thing, but you'll notice the difference.

## Fresh Mozzarella

Fresh mozz has a lot going for it — a clean, milky flavor and a texture that gets soft and pillowy when it melts. It's the traditional choice for a [Backyard Neapolitan Margherita](/recipes/neapolitan-margherita/), and it shows up on our [pesto, mozzarella, and basil pie](/recipes/pesto-mozzarella-basil/) too. When it works, it's worth the extra attention.

The problem is water. Fresh mozzarella can be more than half water by weight, and all of that has to go somewhere during baking. Without a little prep work, it goes straight into your crust.

## How to Stop Fresh Mozzarella from Pooling

The standard advice — and it's good advice — is to drain and dry the cheese before it goes anywhere near your dough. Slice or tear the mozzarella, lay it out on a paper-towel-lined sheet pan or plate, and let it sit uncovered in the fridge for at least an hour. Some cooks go longer. Pat the slices dry before topping.

If you're buying fresh mozz packed in liquid, pour that liquid off as soon as you get home. Don't let it sit in a container of whey until five minutes before you're ready to cook.

A few other things worth knowing:

- **Less is more.** You don't need full coverage. Spread the pieces out with gaps between them — the cheese will spread as it melts.
- **Low-moisture and fresh can share a pie.** A light base of shredded low-moisture gives you the even melt and coverage, and a few torn pieces of fresh on top give you the texture and flavor you're after.
- **Thinner slices release moisture faster.** If you're tearing by hand, smaller pieces work better than thick chunks.

For a plain tomato-forward pie like a [New Haven apizza](/recipes/new-haven-plain-tomato-pie/), skip the fresh entirely — low-moisture is traditional there anyway, and it won't fight the sauce.

## Which One to Default To

If you're not sure, use low-moisture. It's more forgiving, easier to find in most grocery stores, and consistent across a wider range of pies. Fresh mozzarella rewards the extra prep step, but it punishes skipping it.
