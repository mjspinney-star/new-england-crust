A lot of outdoor pizza advice assumes you're managing a live flame. We're not. The Ninja Woodfire is electric — it plugs into an outlet on our deck, and the wood pellets add smoke flavor without doing any of the heating. That changes how you think about the cook, and it means most of the flame-forward tips you'll find online don't quite apply here. This is exactly how we cook our everyday pies — cheese, pepperoni, Chicken Bacon Ranch, and Pesto Mozzarella — and why each step is there.

## Preheat to 550°F — and verify the stone

We set the Ninja to 550°F and give it about 20 minutes to preheat. The air temperature hitting that point is one thing; the stone temperature is another. Stone holds and radiates heat into the bottom of your crust, so it matters more than the ambient reading.

We pull out an infrared thermometer and check the stone directly before every cook. We're looking for around 540°F. If you skip this step, you're guessing — and a cold stone means a pale, doughy bottom no matter how good your dough is. Our post on [how to read your pizza stone with an infrared thermometer](/blog/2026-06-16-reading-your-stone-with-an-infrared-thermometer/) goes deeper on this if you want the details.

**Takeaway:** Don't trust the timer alone. Check the stone temperature before you launch anything.

## Brush the dough with oil and par-bake first

Once the stone is ready, we stretch the dough, brush the top lightly with olive oil, and launch it bare — no sauce, no toppings — directly onto the stone. It goes in for about 1 minute 30 seconds.

The par-bake solves a real problem. Raw dough topped with wet sauce and cheese is fighting itself: the surface is trying to set while steam from the toppings is working against it. Par-baking gives the crust structure before any of that moisture lands on it. The oil helps with color and keeps the surface from drying out too fast.

If your dough isn't relaxed enough to stretch without snapping back, it's not ready. [How to tell when your dough is actually ready to open](/blog/2026-06-08-how-to-tell-when-your-dough-is-actually-ready-to-o/) covers what to look for before you even touch the peel.

**Takeaway:** The par-bake isn't optional for us — it's what keeps the bottom crisp and the structure solid before toppings go on.

## Add toppings, bake about five minutes

We pull the par-baked shell, add sauce and toppings, and send it back in for about 5 minutes. The crust comes out evenly golden with some blistering on the edge. It's not leopard-spotted Neapolitan char — that requires a different heat profile entirely. What we get is consistent color, a set bottom, and toppings that are cooked through without burning.

We don't rotate, turn, or flip the pizza during the bake. The Ninja heats evenly enough at this temperature that it isn't necessary.

How loaded you go with toppings matters. Heavy, wet toppings can stall the cook and soften the crust. If you're looking for topping-forward ideas to try at this heat, [9 pizza night recipes that aren't just Margherita](/blog/pizza-night-recipes-beyond-margherita/) has a range of options.

**Takeaway:** Five minutes is approximate — pull it when the edge is golden and the cheese has color. Keep toppings moderate.

## What makes the dough worth cooking this way

None of this works as well with rushed dough. We use [Caputo Pizzeria 00 flour and cold-ferment the dough for 24 to 48 hours](/blog/72-hour-cold-ferment-dough/). Cold fermentation develops flavor and makes the dough more extensible — easier to stretch without fighting you and better at blistering in the oven.

Our dough goes together in a Cuisinart bread maker on the Dough cycle, then gets balled and sits in the fridge. Low and slow, no shortcuts.

## When the style wants more heat

The Ninja also has a 700°F setting — its maximum. Fully preheated, our stone reads about 695°F on it, and a pie takes about 3 minutes. For high-heat styles like Neapolitan or New Haven apizza, that's the setting to use. We don't use 550°F for everything, and you shouldn't either.
